LESSON 07

When It Goes Wrong

This is the lesson nobody wants to teach but everyone needs.

AI tools confidently say wrong things. Confidently. The same friendly, fluent tone they use for correct answers, they use for completely fabricated ones. Learning to spot this is a survival skill.

The three failure modes

1. Hallucination

The AI invents facts. Names a study that doesn't exist. Quotes a person who never said it. Cites a law that isn't real.

This isn't malice — the AI doesn't know what's true. It produces text that looks like what a true answer would look like. Sometimes that overlaps with reality. Sometimes it doesn't.

2. Confident vagueness

The answer sounds smart but says nothing. "It depends on a variety of factors including market dynamics, regulatory considerations, and stakeholder alignment." That sentence has zero information in it.

This often happens when the AI doesn't actually know but doesn't want to say "I don't know."

3. Outdated information

AIs have training cutoffs. Anything that changed after the cutoff might be answered with old info — confidently.

The three quick checks

Before you trust an AI answer enough to act on it, run these:

Check 1: "Could I verify this?"

If the AI cites a fact, name, statistic, quote, study, law, or specific event — could you check it? If yes, check it before using it. If you can't easily check, treat the claim as unverified.

Check 2: "Is the answer specific or vague?"

If the answer is full of words like "varies," "depends," "many factors," "complex landscape" — that's a signal the AI is hedging because it doesn't actually know. Push it: "Give me specifics, not generalities."

Check 3: "Did the answer match the question?"

Sometimes the AI subtly shifts the topic to something it knows better. You asked about A, it answered about B. Re-read your prompt and re-read the answer. They should be about the same thing.

The danger zone

Medical, legal, financial, and any safety-critical answer needs human expert review. The AI is a starting point, not the answer. "Confidently wrong about your medication dose" is the worst-case scenario for AI misuse.

Pushing back productively

If you suspect the AI is wrong, ask it directly:

"Are you sure about that? What's your source?"

"Could that be outdated? When was your training cutoff?"

"Is there any chance you're hallucinating that name/study/law?"

The AI will often back down and admit uncertainty when challenged. That's useful information.

The "I don't know" prompt

Add this to your prompts when accuracy matters:

"If you're not sure about something, say 'I'm not sure'
 instead of guessing. I'd rather have a partial answer
 than a confident wrong one."

This actually changes how the AI behaves. It'll start flagging its own uncertainty instead of papering over it.

The mindset shift

Treat AI like a brilliant but unreliable assistant. Use it for first drafts, brainstorming, summarizing, exploring options. Verify before acting. Always.

That's not a flaw of AI — it's how to use any tool. You wouldn't trust a calculator without checking the inputs. Same here.
