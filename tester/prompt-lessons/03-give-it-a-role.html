LESSON 03

Give It a Role

One of the most useful tricks in prompting is also one of the most over-prescribed: tell the AI who it should be when answering you.

"You are a pediatric nurse with 20 years of experience.
A new parent asks you about a toddler's fever..."

This is "role-setting" or "persona prompting," and when used well, it changes the AI's vocabulary, depth, tone, and what it considers worth mentioning.

Why it works

Roles compress a lot of context into a few words. "Pediatric nurse" tells the AI: medically careful, parent-friendly, knows what symptoms warrant a doctor visit, won't make jokes. You'd have to say all that explicitly otherwise.

When it backfires

Role-setting goes wrong in three ways:

1. The role is vague

"Act as an expert" — expert in what? Every field has experts. "Expert" alone is just a word.

Bad:  "Act as an expert and review my code."
Good: "Act as a senior backend engineer reviewing a junior dev's
       Python pull request. Look for bugs, style issues, and
       performance problems. Be direct but constructive."

2. The role conflicts with the actual task

Telling the AI to be "a witty stand-up comedian" and then asking it to draft a legal contract gets you a confused, tonally weird contract.

3. You stack too many roles

"You are a Harvard MBA, a Y Combinator partner, a former
McKinsey consultant, and a published author..."

The more roles you stack, the more diluted each becomes. Pick one role that fits the task. One.

When to skip role-setting entirely

For simple tasks — "summarize this paragraph," "fix this typo," "what's 17% of 240" — adding a role just adds noise. Save it for tasks where tone, depth, or domain knowledge matter.

The role-task fit checklist

Before you write a role into your prompt, ask:

  • Does the task have a clear ideal answerer? (e.g. a nutritionist for diet questions, a tax accountant for tax questions). If yes, name that role.
  • Does the role have a known voice? "A patient kindergarten teacher" carries a voice. "A subject matter expert" doesn't.
  • Does the role match the audience? If you're writing for kids, "act as a children's book author" works. "Act as a PhD researcher" doesn't.

A pattern that almost always works

"You are a [specific role] writing for [specific audience].
The reader knows [what they already know]. They want to learn
[what they don't know]. Avoid [known pitfall].

Now: [the actual task]"

That four-line scaffold turns vague requests into reliable output. Try it once and you'll feel the difference.
